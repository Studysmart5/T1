Overview

Core Idea

The heart is a pump made of muscle tissue that needs a source of energy and oxygen, and its pumping action is regulated by an electrical conduction system that coordinates contraction of the different chambers. The heart generates its own electrical signal in the sinus (SA) node, the “natural pacemaker”, which sets the heart rate (normally 60–100 beats/min) and the order of conduction: atria → AV node (brief delay) → bundle of His → bundle branches → ventricles. This overview covers two lectures: Lecture 1 (anatomy and function of the heart's electrical system) and Lecture 2 (cardiac action potentials: non-nodal “fast response” vs nodal “slow response”, their phases and ion conductances, and how antiarrhythmic drugs alter them).

Learning Points

  • The heart is a muscular pump; its pumping is coordinated by an electrical conduction system.
  • The sinus node sits in the upper right atrium and fires regularly, 60–100 times/min under normal conditions; each impulse produces one heartbeat.
  • The sinus signal controls heart rate (number of impulses) and the sequence of conduction across the heart, giving regular, efficient, coordinated beats.
  • The atria are stimulated and contract a fraction of a second before the ventricles, so their blood empties into the ventricles first.
  • At the AV node the impulse is slowed for a very short period, then passes via the bundle of His and the right and left bundle branches to the ventricles.
  • On the EKG: atrial depolarization → P wave; AV-node pause → PR interval; ventricular spread → QRS complex.
  • Non-nodal (fast response) action potentials: atrial and ventricular myocytes and the Purkinje system; they have phases 0–4, a true resting potential and a plateau.
  • Nodal (slow response) action potentials: SA and AV nodes; they show automaticity (spontaneous depolarization) and have no phase 1 or phase 2.
  • Action potentials result from changes in membrane conductance (g) to Na+, K+ and Ca++.
  • Antiarrhythmic drugs block or facilitate these ion movements to prevent or stop arrhythmias.

Key Definitions

  • Sinus node (sinoatrial / SA node): small mass of specialized tissue in the upper right atrium that generates the electrical stimulus regularly; often called the heart's “natural pacemaker”.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) node: the node where impulses are slowed for a very short period before continuing to the ventricles.
  • AV disc: layer of fibrous tissue separating the atria from the ventricles; the wave of electricity is stopped here except at the AV node.
  • Bundle of His (His bundle): conduction pathway from the AV node into the ventricles; divides into the right and left bundle branches (RBB, LBB). The LBB itself splits into the left anterior fascicle (LAF) and left posterior fascicle (LPF).
  • Normal sinus rhythm: rate between 60 and 100 beats per minute.
  • Atrial depolarization: spread of the impulse across the right and left atria, causing them to contract and push blood into the ventricles.
  • P wave: EKG wave generated as the impulse passes through the atria.
  • PR interval: the pause in electrical activity on the EKG while the impulse travels slowly through the AV node.
  • QRS complex: EKG deflection generated as the impulse spreads through the ventricles.
  • Non-nodal action potential (“fast response”): characteristic of atrial and ventricular myocytes and the fast-conducting Purkinje system in the ventricles.
  • Nodal action potential (“slow response”): characteristic of the sinoatrial and AV nodes.
  • Automaticity (pacemaker activity): spontaneous depolarization shown by nodal action potentials.
  • Membrane conductance (g): ease of ion movement (ion currents) into and out of the cell; changes in g alter membrane potential and produce the action potential.
  • Effective refractory period (ERP): period during which the cell cannot be re-excited; the lecture links its lengthening to delayed reactivation of fast-sodium channels (sodium-channel blockers) and to prolonged phase 3 (potassium-channel blockers).
  • “Funny” current (If): current through slow inward Na+ channels during phase 4 of nodal cells.
  • Antiarrhythmic drugs: drugs that block or facilitate ion movement to alter cardiac action potentials and prevent or stop arrhythmias.

Important Concepts

The heart as an electrical pump

  • Like all muscle, the heart needs a source of energy and oxygen to function.
  • The heart's pumping action is regulated by an electrical conduction system that coordinates the contraction of the chambers.
  • The heart generates its own electrical signal, produced by the tiny sinus node in the upper portion of the right atrium.

How the sinus node controls the heartbeat

  • Rate: each electrical impulse generates one heartbeat, so the number of impulses determines the heart rate (normal sinus rhythm 60–100/min; at rest about 60–100 contractions/min, depending on a person's age).
  • Sequence: the signal controls conduction as it spreads, so the heart muscle cells contract in the correct order, giving regular, efficient, coordinated heartbeats.
  • Each ventricular contraction = one heartbeat; atria contract a fraction of a second earlier.

Role of the AV node and AV disc

  • The AV disc stops the wave of electricity everywhere except at the AV node.
  • The slow, controlled passage through the AV node produces a pause (PR interval) that lets the atria contract fully and empty blood into the ventricles before the ventricles contract.

Outcome of ventricular activation

  • Spread through the ventricles causes them to contract, sending blood to the organs of the body (via the left ventricle) and to the lungs (via the right ventricle).

Action potential: general principle

  • Action potentials are determined by movement of different ions (ion currents) into and out of the cell; changes in membrane conductance to these ions change the membrane potential.
  • Drugs that block or facilitate these ion movements alter cardiac action potentials to prevent or stop arrhythmias.

Non-nodal (fast response) action potential

  • Distinguished by a true resting potential (phase 4), a fast depolarization (phase 0), and a prolonged plateau (phase 2).

Nodal (slow response) action potential

  • Shows automaticity and spontaneous depolarization; depolarization is less rapid and action potential duration is shorter than in fast-response cells; no phase 1 or phase 2.
  • Changes in membrane conductance to calcium and potassium alter slow-response action potentials.

Effects of drugs on action potentials

  • Na+-channel blockers (non-nodal): decrease fast inward Na+ movement → lower slope of phase 0 and magnitude of depolarization → principal effect reduced conduction velocity; also increase ERP by delaying reactivation of fast-sodium channels.
  • K+-channel blockers: delay the onset and prolong phase 3 repolarization → longer action potential duration and ERP (stated for both non-nodal and nodal tissue).
  • Ca++-channel blockers (nodal): reduce the slope of phase 4 → slower spontaneous depolarization → lower pacemaker firing rate; reduce the slope of phase 0 → slower conduction velocity in the AV node; lengthen the AV nodal ERP.

Processes / Stages

Conduction sequence of one heartbeat (Lecture 1)

  1. Sinus node (upper right atrium) generates the impulse.
  2. Impulse spreads across the right and left atria (atrial depolarization) → atria contract and push blood into the ventricles; generates the P wave.
  3. The wave reaches the AV disc and is stopped, except at the AV node.
  4. Impulse passes slowly through the AV node → pause on the EKG (PR interval) → atria empty fully into the ventricles.
  5. The AV node conducts the impulse to the His bundle, which passes it to the right and left bundle branches (LBB → LAF and LPF).
  6. The branches send the impulse to the right and left ventricles, which spread it and contract → QRS complex.
  7. Blood is pumped to the body (left ventricle) and lungs (right ventricle).

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Arrhythmias are treated or prevented with antiarrhythmic drugs that block or facilitate ion movements and so alter cardiac action potentials.
  • Na+-channel blockers slow conduction velocity and lengthen the ERP in non-nodal tissue.
  • K+-channel blockers prolong repolarization (phase 3), action potential duration and ERP.
  • Ca++-channel blockers lower the pacemaker firing rate, slow AV-node conduction and lengthen AV nodal ERP.
  • The EKG reflects the electrical sequence: P wave (atria), PR interval (AV-node pause), QRS complex (ventricles).
  • The deliberate AV-node delay allows complete atrial emptying before ventricular contraction.

Exam Focus

  • The heart as a muscular pump regulated by an electrical conduction system; it needs energy and oxygen.
  • Sinus node: location (upper right atrium), rate (60–100/min), “natural pacemaker”, controls both rate and sequence.
  • Full conduction pathway: SA node → atria → AV node (slowed) → His bundle → right/left bundle branches (LAF, LPF) → ventricles.
  • Why the AV node delays the impulse; the role of the AV disc.
  • EKG correlates: P wave, PR interval, QRS complex.
  • Figure abbreviations: SN, AVN, RA, LA, RV, LV, TV, MV, RBB, LBB, LAF, LPF.
  • Right ventricle → lungs; left ventricle → body.
  • Non-nodal vs nodal action potentials: locations, phases present, resting potential vs spontaneous depolarization, speed, duration.
  • Phase-by-phase ion conductances (0–4) for non-nodal cells and (0, 3, 4) for nodal cells; “funny” currents (If).
  • Drug effects: Na+-channel blockers (phase 0, conduction velocity, ERP); K+-channel blockers (phase 3, duration, ERP); Ca++-channel blockers (phase 4 and 0, pacemaker rate, AV conduction, ERP).
